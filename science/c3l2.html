Part 2 • Easy Lesson

Why Use More Than One Property?

Mineralogists are scientists who study the distribution of minerals, mineral properties, and their uses.

They use simple tests and compare physical and chemical properties to classify an unknown mineral.

One Property

Can match several minerals or vary within one mineral.

Several Properties

Create a stronger group of clues for the mineral's identity.

⭐ Remember: One clue can mislead; several clues work together.
Launch Lab

Grow Crystals from Solution

  1. Complete a lab safety form. Label the beakers.
  2. Add salt to 20 mL hot—not boiling—water until no more dissolves. Repeat with alum, Epsom salt, and washing soda.
  3. With a clean dropper for each solution, remove 5 mL. Put 5–10 drops of each on a jar lid; make a fifth mixed puddle with 2 drops of each.
  4. Keep the lid warm and check at regular times for two days.

Compare whether crystals formed, whether they looked the same, and which property helped tell them apart.

⭐ Remember: Different crystals can be compared by observable properties.
Color

Color Alone Is Not Reliable

Different Minerals, Same Color

Olivine and pyroxene are both green.

One Mineral, Many Colors

Quartz can be clear, white, smoky gray, purple, orange, or pink. Tourmaline can be yellow, pink, green, or blue.

Different chemical impurities—including iron, chromium, or manganese—can cause color variations.

Key Concept Check: Quartz cannot be classified by color alone because the same mineral can appear in several colors.

⭐ Remember: Same color can mean different minerals; one mineral can have different colors.
Luster

How Light Meets the Surface

Luster is the way a mineral reflects or absorbs light at its surface.

  • Metallic: the shiniest luster; metals such as copper, silver, and gold reflect light.
  • Nonmetallic: may be shiny but is not reflective like metal.

Other luster words: brilliant, waxy, silky, pearly, vitreous (glassy), earthy, and dull.

Luster is directly related to chemical composition. Figure 9 contrasts the nonmetallic-looking talc surface with the reflective, metallic-looking hematite surface.

⭐ Remember: Luster describes reflection or absorption of light at the mineral's surface.
Streak

The Color of the Powder

Streak is the color of a mineral in powdered form.

Rub a mineral across an unglazed porcelain scratch plate. A colored streak may remain.

  • Useful only for minerals softer than porcelain.
  • Nonmetallic minerals generally make a white streak.
  • Metallic minerals often make characteristic streak colors.
⭐ Remember: Streak tests powder color, not the outside color of the sample.

⭐ Remember: Hematite's surface color varies, but its reddish/brown streak stays the same.
Hardness

Resistance to Scratching

Hardness is the resistance of a mineral to being scratched.

German mineralogist Friedrich Mohs developed the Mohs hardness scale from 1 to 10.

1 = Softest

Talc has hardness 1.

10 = Hardest

Diamond has hardness 10.

A harder material scratches a softer material.

⭐ Remember: Higher Mohs number = greater resistance to scratching.

Using the Scale

Read a Scratch Test

  • Quartz (7) scratches feldspar (6), calcite (3), and talc (1).
  • Quartz does not scratch topaz (8), corundum (9), or diamond (10).
  • A mineral that scratches a penny (3.5) but not quartz (7) has hardness between 3.5 and 7.
  • A mineral scratched by a fingernail has hardness below 2.5.
Review problem: A hardness-6 mineral scratches glass (5.5) and a penny (3.5). A hardness-4 mineral scratches the penny but does not scratch the glass.
⭐ Remember: If X scratches Y, X is harder than Y.
Cleavage and Fracture

How a Mineral Breaks

The arrangement of atoms or ions and the strength of chemical bonds determine how a mineral breaks. Minerals break where bonds are weakest.

Cleavage

Smooth, flat break surfaces.

Fracture

Uneven break surfaces.

⭐ Remember: Cleavage is flat; fracture is uneven.
Cleavage

Weak Bonds Make Flat Surfaces

If a mineral breaks with smooth, flat surfaces, it has cleavage.

Calcite breaks along three directions of cleavage, identified as three sets of parallel sides.

In the MiniLab, each set of parallel sides equals one cleavage direction.

Word origin in the material: cleavage comes from Old English cleofan, meaning “to split, separate.”

⭐ Remember: Count sets of parallel sides to count cleavage directions.
Fracture

Equal Bonds Break Unevenly

If a mineral breaks and forms uneven surfaces, it has fracture.

Quartz breaks unevenly because its bonds are equally strong in all directions. It can break like thick glass with smooth, curved surfaces.

Fracture patterns can be unpredictable. Asbestos can break into splinters or fibers.

⭐ Remember: Fracture does not make the repeated flat planes seen in cleavage.
MiniLab

Classify the Break Pattern

  1. Complete a lab safety form and obtain the samples from the teacher.
  2. Separate samples into cleavage and fracture groups.
  3. For cleavage, count sets of parallel sides; each set equals one direction. Record observations.
  4. For samples without cleavage, describe and record the surfaces.
  5. Use the break pattern as another clue to identity.

Why useful? Minerals break in patterns controlled by atomic arrangement and bond strength, so cleavage or fracture can help distinguish them.

⭐ Remember: Observe flat planes, parallel sides, or uneven surfaces.
Density

Mass Compared with Volume

Density equals the mass of an object divided by its volume.

Density = Mass ÷ Volume   (g/cm³)

If two minerals have about the same volume, the one that feels heavier has more mass and therefore greater density.

The bowling ball is more dense than a same-size volleyball because its mass is greater.

⭐ Remember: Same volume + more mass = greater density.
Skill Practice • Measure

Find a Mineral's Density

  1. Measure mineral mass with a balance.
  2. Record the exact starting water volume—about 50 mL—in a 100-mL graduated cylinder.
  3. Lower the tied mineral below the surface without touching the bottom; record final water volume.
  4. Mineral volume = final volume − starting volume.
  5. Density = mass ÷ mineral volume.

For water displacement, 1 mL = 1 cm³. Remove the mineral and repeat for each sample. Density is more useful than color for identifying a particular mineral because color can vary.

⭐ Remember: Measure mass, find displaced volume, then divide.
Special Properties

Touch, Reaction, and Odor

Texture

Graphite feels greasy. Talc feels smooth.

Reaction

Calcite fizzes and produces a gas with hydrochloric acid.

Odor

Sulfur smells like a match. Kaolinite smells like clay.

These distinctive properties can add useful evidence to the identification.

⭐ Remember: Special clues may be felt, observed in a reaction, or smelled.
Special Properties

Glow or Magnetism

Fluorescence is a mineral's ability to glow when exposed to ultraviolet light. Calcite and fluorite can fluoresce.

Some minerals are magnetic. Magnetite is magnetic because iron is present in its chemical formula.

⭐ Remember: Fluorescence = glow under ultraviolet light; magnetite = magnetic.

Part 7 • Final 4-Minute Review

Remember These Before the Test

  • Use more than one property.
  • Color varies; streak can stay consistent.
  • Luster = surface light behavior.
  • Hardness = scratch resistance.
  • Mohs: quartz 7, glass 5.5, penny 3.5, fingernail 2.5.
  • Cleavage = smooth, flat; fracture = uneven.
  • Density = mass ÷ volume.
  • Volume by water displacement: final − initial.
  • Fluorescence = UV glow; magnetite is magnetic.
  • Symmetrical crystal shapes indicate orderly atoms.
