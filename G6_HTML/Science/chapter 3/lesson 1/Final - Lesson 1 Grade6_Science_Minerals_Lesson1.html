Part 2 • Easy Lesson

Rock or Mineral?

Rocks and minerals are often mistaken for one another.

Rock

Commonly contains two or more minerals.

Mineral

Made of one uniform substance.

The Launch Lab groups objects by common physical properties, then separates one-uniform-substance objects from objects containing two or more substances.

⭐ Remember: A rock commonly has two or more minerals; a mineral is one uniform substance.
The Core Definition

Five Characteristics of a Mineral

A mineral is a naturally occurring, inorganic solid with a definite chemical composition and an orderly arrangement of atoms or ions.

1Naturally occurring
2Inorganic
3Solid
4Definite chemical composition
5Orderly arrangement of atoms or ions
⭐ Remember: A substance must have all five characteristics to be a mineral.
Characteristics 1 and 4

Natural and Chemically Definite

Naturally Occurring

Minerals occur naturally and are not made in a laboratory. About 4,000 minerals occur on Earth; about 30 are common; 10 are called rock-forming minerals.

Examples: quartz, feldspar, and olivine.

Definite Composition

Hematite is Fe₂O₃: two parts iron and three parts oxygen.

Silver (Ag) and sulfur (S) are one-element native elements. Potassium feldspar combines several elements.

⭐ Remember: Each mineral occurs naturally and has its own definite chemical composition.
Characteristic 5

Crystalline Form

The internal arrangement of atoms or ions determines crystal shape.

  • Halite: sodium and chlorine ions repeat in three dimensions, making a cubic crystal pattern.
  • Glass: silicon and oxygen atoms do not have an orderly, repeating arrangement.

Lesson Review idea: Crystal shape reflects the internal arrangement of atoms or ions.

⭐ Remember: Repeating in three directions makes a crystal.
Characteristics 2 and 3

Solid and Inorganic

Solid

A solid has tightly packed atoms or ions and a definite shape and volume.

All minerals are solids, but not all solids are minerals. Solids without crystal form, liquids, and gases are not minerals.

Inorganic

Minerals are not from biologic origins.

Yet some minerals can form as a result of organic processes: marine organisms can extract dissolved solids from seawater and make shells.

Gypsum can form as water evaporates and leaves dissolved solids behind. Its visible solid crystal form is evidence that it has mineral characteristics.

⭐ Remember: A mineral is a crystalline solid and is inorganic.
The Structure of Minerals

Shape Gives a Clue

Minerals have definite crystalline form, and shape reflects the internal arrangement of atoms or ions.

  • Quartz (SiO₂): long crystals with distinctly pointed ends.
  • Calcite (CaCO₃): somewhat diamond shaped, with sets of parallel sides.

Geologists can infer the internal arrangement of atoms or ions from a mineral's shape. Shape helps identification, but the text asks whether structure alone is enough.

⭐ Remember: Crystal shape is an outside clue to the inside arrangement.
Crystal Shape

Galena and Tiny Crystals

Galena (PbS)

Lead and sulfur ions form cubes. The angles between them are 90°, so galena has a characteristic cubic shape.

Size and Growth

Most minerals grow in tiny clusters. Under the right conditions and with time to grow, a crystal can develop a characteristic, large, well-developed shape.

Scientists use scanning electron microscope images to reveal orderly arrangements in tiny crystals. The MiniLab compares halite (rock salt), a common mineral in table salt, with Epsom salt (magnesium sulfate), found in mineral water. Students observe and draw their shapes; the material does not state the exact Epsom salt shape.

⭐ Remember: Even tiny crystals have order; time and the right conditions allow larger shapes.
Common Minerals

Two Rock-Forming Families

Silicate: a member of the mineral group that has silicon and oxygen in its crystal structure.

SilicatesNonsilicates
Quartz, potassium feldspar, plagioclase feldspar, olivine, pyroxene, amphibole, micaCalcite, halite
Contain silicon and oxygenDo not contain silicon

Oxygen and silicon are the two most abundant elements in Earth's crust. Pure quartz contains only oxygen and silicon. Feldspar is the most common silicate mineral in Earth's crust.

⭐ Remember: Silicates contain silicon and oxygen; nonsilicates do not contain silicon.
How Minerals Form

One Process: Crystallization

Crystallization is the formation of crystals. It occurs when particles dissolved in a liquid or a melt solidify and form crystals.

Particles in a liquid or melt
→
Particles solidify
→
Crystals form

Minerals can crystallize from hot or cool solutions and as molten rock cools. Their chemical and physical properties help geologists infer the environment where they formed.

⭐ Remember: Different environments, same process—crystallization.
Minerals from Cool Solutions

Water Moves and Dissolves

Rain or snowmelt
→
Water moves through ground or over the surface
→
Water dissolves minerals

Water picks up elements such as potassium, calcium, iron, and silicon from rock and soil. These become dissolved solids.

Dissolve: to disperse or disappear in solution.

⭐ Remember: Moving water can carry dissolved solids from rock and soil.
Cool Solutions

Evaporation or Organisms

Dry Conditions

Water can hold only a certain amount of dissolved solids. As water evaporates, solids crystallize out. Halite formed when water from a shallow lake evaporated.

Seawater

Seawater can become saturated—it cannot hold more salt. Certain marine organisms remove salts to make protective shells or build a reef.

⭐ Remember: Cool-water minerals can form by evaporation or by organisms removing dissolved salts.
Minerals from Hot Solutions

Hot Water Fills Cracks

Surface water enters cracks
→
Reaches deep, hot environments
→
Dissolved solids crystallize

Hot solutions can carry large concentrations of dissolved solids. When conditions are right, gold crystallizes from hot-water solution and fills cracks in rock.

The giant gypsum crystals in Mexico's Cave of Crystals are an extreme example of crystals forming from solids dissolved in superheated water.

⭐ Remember: A gold vein forms when gold crystallizes from hot water and fills rock cracks.
Minerals from Molten Rock

Magma, Lava, and Ash

Magma

Molten rock stored beneath Earth's surface.

Lava or Ash

Molten rock that erupts on or near Earth's surface.

As lava or ash cools above ground, or magma cools underground, atoms and ions arrange themselves and form mineral crystals.

Word origin in the material: lava comes from Latin lavare, meaning “to wash.”

⭐ Remember: Beneath = magma; erupted on or near the surface = lava or ash.
Cooling Rate

Fast Makes Small; Slow Makes Large

Fast Cooling

Lava cools quickly on or near the surface → small crystals, some barely visible.

Slow Cooling

Magma cools and crystallizes slowly underground → sometimes large crystals.

In Figure 8, basalt has very small crystals, so it cooled more quickly than the andesite with larger crystals.

⭐ Remember: Fast cooling → small crystals; slow cooling → larger crystals.
Changes in Minerals

New Conditions, New Minerals

Stable deep underground under high pressure and temperature
→
Tectonic activity uplifts minerals
→
Surface conditions and erosion break them down
→
New minerals form

Water, wind, and ice are agents of erosion named in the material.

Olivine forms under high temperatures and pressures; quartz forms in less extreme conditions. A rock containing both is highly unlikely.

⭐ Remember: A change in pressure, temperature, and erosion can lead to new minerals.

Lesson Review • Questions 1–7

Learn the Review Answers

1. “Crystallization occurs when particles dissolved in a liquid or a melt solidify and form crystals.”

2. Molten rock below Earth's surface = magma.

3. A silicate contains silicon and oxygen.

4. A shallow lake in dry conditions forms minerals by evaporation.

5. Evaporation leaves dissolved solids; seawater organisms remove dissolved salts; magma forms crystals as it cools.

6. Crystallization from magma is caused by cooling.

7. Three environments: cool solutions, hot solutions, molten rock.

Careers in Science

Platinum Holds Clues

Platinum is a precious metal, rarer than gold and silver. Most is mined in three locations named in the material: Montana, Russia, and South Africa.

Ed Mathez, a geologist at the American Museum of Natural History, studies platinum ore deposits and layered igneous rocks in South Africa's Bushveld Igneous Complex for clues to Earth's past.

How the Layers Formed

Magma cooled and crystallized within Earth's crust. Dense lower layers contain iron-, sulfur-, and chromium-rich sulfide deposits with tiny platinum concentrations.

How Much Platinum?

The densest lower layers average 6–8 ppm. For every 1 million atoms, only about 6 are platinum.

Its durability and shiny surface make platinum popular for jewelry. In catalytic converters, platinum helps convert toxic gas emissions into less harmful gases.

⭐ Remember: Platinum deposits help Mathez study both rare metals and Earth's past.
